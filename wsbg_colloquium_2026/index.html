Supervisor
Thu Phuong Truong

Paper presentation

Faculty member

Abstract

Carbon pricing is intended to influence firms’ economic decisions and facilitate the reallocation of capital toward a low-carbon economy. Its effectiveness, however, depends partly on whether carbon prices convey economically meaningful information that is incorporated into financial asset prices. This issue is particularly important in New Zealand, where the New Zealand Emissions Trading Scheme (NZ ETS) is the country’s primary market-based climate policy instrument and has undergone substantial institutional change, including its delinking from international Kyoto markets in 2015 and the introduction of government auctions in 2021.

This study examines three related questions: whether unanticipated carbon price shocks are reflected in the stock returns of New Zealand listed firms; whether this relationship changes as the institutional design and domestic relevance of the NZ ETS evolve; and whether firms’ responses differ across industries and levels of carbon intensity.

Using daily data for 172 firms listed on the New Zealand Exchange (NZX) from May 2010 to May 2025, together with daily New Zealand Unit (NZU) spot prices, the study constructs unanticipated carbon price shocks as standardized innovations from an AR(7)-EGARCH(1,1) model. Firm fixed-effects panel regressions examine contemporaneous and lagged stock-market responses while controlling for domestic and international financial and macroeconomic factors. Structural-break tests compare the internationally linked and post-delinking regimes, with a three-regime specification further distinguishing the post-delinking domestic-market period from the auction era. Industry and carbon-intensity analyses examine heterogeneous firm exposure.

Preliminary findings indicate that unanticipated NZU price shocks contain value-relevant information for New Zealand equity investors. Stock-market responses are positive and persistent, with the strongest effects often appearing with a one-day lag, while negative shocks generate stronger average responses than positive shocks. Importantly, the carbon-equity relationship is strongly regime-dependent: carbon price shocks have limited explanatory power during the internationally linked period, become substantially more informative following delinking, and remain relevant, although more moderately, during the auction era. The response also varies across industries and firms with different carbon intensities.

The study contributes to the climate-finance literature by providing evidence from a small, policy-driven emissions trading system and showing that the financial-market relevance of carbon prices depends not only on carbon-price movements themselves but also on the institutional framework that determines their informational content. It highlights the importance of credible, domestically informative carbon-price signals for the transmission of climate policy into financial markets.

Keywords: Carbon Pricing; Stock Returns; Emissions Trading Schemes; Institutional Credibility; Climate Finance.

Abstract

The Net-Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) members commit to align lending and investment portfolios with net-zero emissions by 2050. Using euro-area credit registry data, Sastry et al. (2024) find no evidence that joining NZBA increases exposure to renewables or reduces exposure to high-emission sectors, as the commitment raises ESG ratings. Bingler et al. (2023, 2024) reach a similar conclusion in a broader sample: vague net-zero commitments are followed by faster emissions growth, whereas specific and costly commitments are associated with better outcomes. This distinction between cheap talk and costly, validated signalling is now central to the greenwashing literature, which treats validated commitments as the correction for vague ones (Furrer et al., 2012; Giannetti et al., 2023). This study examines whether costly validation alone produces better outcomes or requires operational disclosure to do so.

The existing empirical literature proxies credibility with single-dimensional measures. For example, framework-based content scoring captures structured alignment, mostly against the TCFD framework, but not whether commitments are quantitatively substantiated (Bui et al., 2020; Caby et al., 2022; Demaria & Rigot, 2021; Pitrakkos & Maroun, 2020). Third-party ratings such as CDP and SBTi provide external validation but on narrow criteria. CDP uses scoring points that reward the completeness of disclosure rather than target ambition, and SBTi validates methodological alignment without capturing implementation (Matsumura et al., 2014; Nishitani et al., 2020; Ott et al., 2017). Readability metrics capture linguistic clarity but not disclosure substance (Bingler et al., 2022; Loughran and McDonald, 2014; Nik Ahmad and Hossain, 2019). More methods use computational text analysis to extract specific claim types with increasing precision (Bingler & Colesanti Senni, 2022; Colesanti Senni et al., 2024) without framework compliance or third-party validation.

Each of these traditions measures one facet of credibility. None captures the construct as defined by international standards, which require credible transition plans to include governance, ambition, implementation, metrics, transparency, and engagement (FSB, 2025; IFRS S2, 2023; NGFS & Banque de France, 2023; OECD, 2022). No single indicator fully reflects this definition. Empirical studies cannot yet separate banks that build credibility across multiple disclosure attributes from banks that satisfy only a single criterion. Nevertheless, they cannot differentiate between disclosures and real actions.

My study addresses this measurement gap by analysing 91 NZBA member banks across 30 countries over 2020–2022. It reflects the voluntary disclosure era before the incorporation of the TCFD into IFRS S2, which took effect in 2024. This period captures the voluntary and normative pressures that shape banks' climate commitments rather than mandatory reporting requirements, so cross-bank variation reflects choices about how to construct credibility.

How Regulatory Pressures Shape Environmental Performance of MNEs in Carbon Intensive Industries: The Moderating Roles of Normative Pressures, Cognitive Pressures, and Green Innovation

Supervisor
Revti Raman
Contribution

Abstract

Introduction

Despite growing research on environmental sustainability in international business and strategy, the role of multinational enterprises (MNEs) in addressing environmental performance challenges remains contested (Sharma, 2026). While some MNEs lead and champion environmental sustainability initiatives, others resort to greenwashing practices and exploit pollution havens (Bu et al., 2025; Genin & Bu, 2026). Regulations play an important role in influencing MNEs to enhance their environmental performance. However, the role of regulations is also debated. Some scholars argue that stringent environmental regulations can prompt firms to improve environmental performance, whereas others find that MNEs may respond to regulatory pressures by shifting pollution, engaging in symbolic compliance, or merely meeting minimum legal requirements (Aragòn-Correa et al., 2020; Genin & Bu, 2026; Xiong et al., 2025).

This study aims to contribute to the debate on the mixed effects of regulatory pressures by examining how these pressures shape the environmental performance of MNEs. It argues that the effect of regulatory pressures on environmental performance is contingent on normative pressures, cognitive pressures, and a firm’s green innovation capability.

This study amis to address three research questions: (1) How do regulatory pressures affect the environmental performance of MNEs? (2) Do normative and cognitive pressures moderate the relationship between regulatory pressures and environmental performance? (3) Does green innovation moderate the relationship between regulatory pressures and environmental performance? (Sharma, 2025; Shui et al., 2025).

Research Contribution

Existing studies report mixed findings regarding the role of regulatory pressures in improving environmental performance, with some studies highlighting environmental improvement and others identifying pollution shifting, institutional arbitrage, and symbolic compliance (Dau et al., 2022; Genin & Bu, 2026; Milosevic et al., 2023). Drawing on institutional theory, this study contributes to the debate by identifying normative and cognitive pressures as key boundary conditions that shape regulatory effectiveness. Furthermore, drawing on the natural resource-based view (Hart, 1995; Hart & Dowell, 2011), the study incorporates green innovation as a firm-level capability explaining why MNEs facing similar regulatory pressures may experience divergent environmental performance (Kano et al., 2025; Sharma, 2025). By focusing on MNEs, the study also provides context-sensitive insights into how multinational operations influence the relationship between regulatory pressures and environmental performance (Zaheer, 2025).

Methodology

This study adopts a quantitative, non-experimental, explanatory longitudinal panel research design. The empirical analysis is based on a sample of 151 Fortune Global 500 MNEs operating in carbon-intensive industries and headquartered in 28 countries over the period 2006-2026. Environmental performance is measured using the ASSET4 Environmental Pillar Score. Regulatory pressure is measured using the Environmental Performance Index (EPI), normative pressure using internet search attention to environmental pollution from Google Trends and Baidu Index, cognitive pressure using industry-level diffusion of ISO 14001 certification combined with industry concentration, and green innovation using green patent applications identified through the WIPO IPC Green Inventory (Genin & Bu, 2026; Hartmann et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Fixed-effects panel regression models will be employed to test the proposed hypotheses (Wooldridge, 2010).

Findings

Consistent with institutional theory and the NRBV, this study expects that regulatory pressures alone will not generate uniform environmental performance among MNEs. Instead, Its effects depend on the normative pressures, cognitive pressures, and green innovation capabilities.

Keywords: MNEs, Institutional Pressures, Environmental Performance, Green Innovation, Institutional Theory, Natural Resource-based View

Abstract

Introduction

Organisations that set out to reduce gender inequality frequently reproduce it. Diversity initiatives are introduced with inclusive intent yet often leave the arrangements that produced exclusion intact (Ely & Meyerson, 2000; Williams et al., 2012). Explaining that gap between intention and consequence requires an account of power. Acker’s (1990) theory of ‘Gendered Organisations’ remains an influential framework for locating gender inequality in organisational life, and Acker herself insisted that its five gendering processes are interconnected. Yet she placed power explicitly within only the first process. Moreover, the framework has been used mostly to document inequalities and prove that organisations are indeed gendered rather than to explain who the relevant actors are, and where the potential for long-term transformation lies (Bates, 2022; Tafvelin & Keisu, 2024). This study examines how power is enacted and how it may shift from control-based, “power-over” to more inclusive “power-with” approaches.

The research questions are: (1) How do leadership practices in comparatively gender-equal organisations adopt power-with (empowerment) versus power-over (control) strategies? (2) How does power enactment influence gender inclusion? and (3) What does this reveal about organisational networks as mechanisms of gendered inclusion and exclusion?

Contribution

The study contributes to Acker’s (1990) theory of ‘Gendered Organisations’ by offering alternative perspectives on organisational behaviour, leadership, gendered power, and power enactment. It argues that power operates across, rather than within a single, gendering process and becomes observable through organisational mechanisms. Organisational networks provide a useful lens because they are formal and informal structures, embody cultural assumptions about who belongs, provide sites of everyday interaction, and shape the resources through which gendered identities are performed. Mechanisms, on this account, reveal how the interconnections Acker theorised operate in organisational life. Repositioning power as operating across the gendering processes shifts the question from where inequality is located to how it travels, who enacts it and where it might be interrupted. The study aims to examine role model organisations and identify their conditions under which power dynamics contribute to offering pathways to greater gender inclusion.

Methodology

I have conducted a systematic literature review with 100 papers, exploring how authors who draw on Acker’s theory of ‘Gendered Organisations’ have conceptualised power and what this reveals about organisational networks as mechanisms of gendered inclusion or exclusion.

Furthermore, I will apply a combination of qualitative expert interviews and a multiple case study design, analysing comparatively gender-equal organisations in New Zealand. Through purposeful sampling the study will focus on large private organisations that have a GenderTick accreditation. Data will be collected through open interviews with leaders, supplemented by document analysis and potentially observations and focus groups, depending on the level of access the case study organisations are granting. The analysis will combine inductive open coding, allowing categories to emerge naturally from the data, and deductive pre-established coding. NVivo will be used for coding, followed by theoretical mapping.

Expected Findings

The study expects to identify how leaders enact power-with versus power-over approaches to achieve gender inclusion, and how they describe mechanisms to enact power, e.g. through formal or informal networks. I believe that organisations that demonstrate strong efforts towards gender equality will likely apply power-with leadership approaches (an empowering and inclusive way of doing power). The way power is enacted will influence female employees’ access to information, resources, support, opportunities, and networks.

Keywords: Gendered organisations; power enactment; gender inclusion; leadership; organisational networks

Abstract

Structural transformation is characterized by a declining agricultural employment share and the reallocation of resources across sectors. Yet this process is markedly gendered, with women in developing countries disproportionately concentrated in agriculture. This paper studies how fertility shapes gendered structural transformation in China. In particular, we examine whether fertility affects women’s and men’s sectoral choices differently, and whether these differences matter for structural transformation and aggregate productivity.

Empirical analysis shows that higher fertility is associated with rural women being more likely to work in agriculture and less likely to migrate to urban nonagricultural sectors. These effects are much weaker for men, highlighting the gender-based labor allocation associated with fertility constraints.

To quantify the aggregate implications, we develop a multi-sector general equilibrium model in which households jointly choose the quantity and quality of children, consumption, and sector of employment. Rural households choose among agriculture, rural nonagriculture, and urban nonagricultural migration. The key mechanism is that childrearing time costs are gender- and sector-specific, making nonagricultural employment relatively more costly than agricultural work for women with children. The model also incorporates China’s household registration system and the one-child policy.

Counterfactual analysis shows that reducing women’s childrearing burden increases female migration, shifts women out of agriculture, and raises aggregate productivity. Sectoral labor reallocation accounts for about 18% of the productivity gain, with most of this effect operating through women’s labor allocation. In contrast, removing the one-child policy raises fertility, reduces female migration, and lowers productivity. Additional counterfactuals show that lower migration costs and improvements in rural human capital also promote labor reallocation and productivity growth, with particularly strong effects on female labor allocation.

The paper contributes to the literature on structural transformation by introducing fertility as an endogenous source of gender-specific labor-allocation frictions. It highlights childrearing costs as an important link between household fertility decisions, female labor mobility, and aggregate productivity.

Keywords: Fertility, Gendered Labor Allocation, Structural Transformation, Agriculture

The use of legal performance-enhancing supplements by young adult male non-professional gym users: A social marketing challenge

Abstract

Commercial gyms are no longer merely places to exercise; they are social and commercial spaces where people pursue body ideals and the consumption of “beneficial” products is facilitated (Sassatelli, 2010). Such products include legal performance-enhancing supplements (legal PES) which are consumed by a significant proportion of young adult males gym users (Ruano & Teixeira, 2020). Legal PES products such as protein powder, creatine, and pre-workout supplements, are purposefully ingested to increase strength, muscle mass, and endurance (Maughan et al., 2018).

Although legally available and widely regarded as safe, legal PES are not risk-free as users often exceed recommended intakes and rarely seek medical advice for adverse effects (Pilegaard et al., 2022). Legal PES are promoted both commercially, through marketing messages emphasising performance and appearance (Catalani et al., 2021), and interpersonally, through peer encouragement and casual advice from friends and trainers (Druker & Gesser-Edelsburg, 2017; Lopes et al., 2024), yet rarely is information conveyed about risks, proper use or long-term effects. In addition, most research on performance-enhancing substances concentrates on the use of steroids and illegal substance use by competitive athletes and professional bodybuilders, leaving a significant gap in the understanding of legal PES use among young males who train recreationally (Hilkens et al., 2021; Sundgot-Borgen et al., 2022).

Legal PES use therefore sits at the intersection of commercial consumption, nutrition and masculine identity (Atkinson, 2007). Previous studies on supplement use have typically applied single theoretical perspectives, such as the Theory of Reasoned Action (Bayır et al., 2024), the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1991; Haubenstricker et al., 2023) or a social media–body image pathway (Hilkens et al., 2021), rather than a holistic framework examining how individual, social and environmental factors influence each other. Furthermore, although social marketing has addressed behaviours such as healthy eating and alcohol use (Carins & Rundle-Thiele, 2014; Dietrich et al., 2016), legal PES use has received minimal attention from this perspective.

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) is well suited to addressing this gap. Through reciprocal determinism, it holds that behaviour results from continuous, three-way interaction between the Person (motivation, knowledge, self-image), the Environment (peers, media, marketing, gym culture), and Behaviour itself (Bandura, 1986). Consequently, this exploratory qualitative study will investigate legal PES use among young adult male gym users through the theoretical lens of SCT.

Rather than testing hypotheses, it is guided by three objectives:

RO1: To explore how the Person domain shapes and is shaped by legal PES use among young adult male gym users (Behaviour domain)

RO2: To explore how the Environment domain shapes and is shaped by legal PES use among young adult male gym users (Behaviour domain)

RO3: To explore how the Person domain shapes and is shaped by the Environment domain with regard to legal PES use among young adult male gym users.

Research Contribution

The study will elucidate reciprocal determinism in SCT and, through theory synthesis (Jaakkola, 2020), inform more effective social marketing approaches to legal PES use.

Methodology

A three-phase sequential design (24–30 semi-structured interviews, four focus groups and participatory workshops) will use purposive sampling of English-speaking, Wellington-based males aged 18–35 (stratified 18–25 and 26–35) who attend a commercial gym at least weekly and have used legal PES. Data will be analysed using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Expected Findings

The study is expected to show how personal motivations, social norms and gym culture shape legal PES use, and these findings will then inform the development of participant-informed messaging strategies.

Keywords: legal performance-enhancing supplements; Social Cognitive Theory; reciprocal determinism; social marketing; young adult male gym users

Valuation of an Irreversible Investment under Markov Regime Switching

Supervisor
Toby Daglish
Contribution
Paper presentation
Session chair
Janet Davey
Faculty member

Abstract

Introduction

Most investments are partly irreversible, uncertain, and flexible in timing. Here, the simple net present value rule fails (Dixit and Pindyck 1994). Investing today gives up the option to wait, so the project must be worth more than its cost. McDonald and Siegel (1986) show this hurdle can be about twice the cost and rises with uncertainty. Drift and volatility, often assumed fixed, shift with the business cycle and policy. Regime-switching models let them follow a finite-state Markov chain, but assume the firm observes the regime (Driffill et al. 2003; Guo et al. 2005; Driffill et al. 2013; Boomsma et al. 2012). Learning models hide the state, and the firm invests when its belief crosses a threshold; but the hidden state never changes (Thijssen et al. 2004; Harrison and Sunar 2015; Shibata 2008; Grenadier and Malenko 2010).

Research Contribution

A regime that is both hidden and switching has little attention. We fill this gap by combining regime switching with real options and using the Wonham filter, which gives exact belief dynamics for a hidden Markov chain. The investment threshold then depends on beliefs, and we show how learning and regime persistence shape investment timing.

Methodology

The project cash flow follows a geometric Brownian motion whose drift and volatility depend on the current regime. There are four regimes, and switches between them follow a continuous-time Markov chain. We group the regimes into a good class and a bad class. Within each class, one regime is persistent and the other is transient. The firm sees the cash flow but not the regime.

The firm forms a belief (a probability) for each regime. These beliefs follow the Wonham filter (Wonham, 1964). The filter has two parts. The first part moves beliefs according to the regime switching rates. The second part updates beliefs using the gap between the observed cash flow growth and the growth the firm expected.

In this first case, the two regimes in each class share the same drift and volatility. Volatility reveals the class but not the regime within it. The second part of the filter then drops out, and beliefs follow a closed-form path over time. We also assume no jumps within a class and fixed jump ratios.

After investment, the firm receives the cash flow in perpetuity. The project value equals the cash flow times a multiple that depends on beliefs. We solve this multiple in closed form. Before investment, the option value in each class solves a partial differential equation in the log cash flow and the belief. The two equations are coupled, because a jump moves the firm to the other class. At the trigger, value matching and smooth pasting hold.

We solve the system with finite differences on a grid of log cash flow and belief. A projected SOR solver handles the early-exercise condition, and fixed-point iteration handles the coupling between classes. The study at this stage is theoretical and uses no empirical data. Illustrative parameter values show how the model behaves.

Findings

The project value multiple is linear in beliefs. At low cash flow, the option value is close to zero. Above the trigger, it rises steeply. The firm in the bad class waits for a higher cash flow than the firm in the good class. The exercise boundary depends on beliefs and splits the state space into action, inaction and transient regions. In the good class, more belief in the persistent best regime lowers the trigger, so the firm invests sooner. In the bad class, more belief in the persistent worst regime raises the trigger, so the firm waits longer. Smooth pasting holds numerically, which supports the accuracy of the solution.

Future work

In the next stage, we will restore the second part of the filter so that beliefs react to the observed cash flow. Beliefs then become random, and adds a second-order term to the equation, so the numerical problem changes.

Keywords: Real Options, Markov Switching, Bayesian Approach, Dynamic Programming, Policy Uncertainty

Long-run Accounting Performance of Divestments and Its Association with Investors’ Perceptions of Market Returns

Abstract

Corporations reconfigure their business portfolios through acquisitions and divestments to maximise shareholders' value. Corporations pursue acquisitions to expand or diversify their operations. However, continuous expansion and diversification may no longer maximise shareholder value, and corporations may reach a point where they need to reconsider their business structure. Divestment allows corporations to reallocate resources for future growth.

Divestments used to be opportunistic moves, but now they are strategic decisions focused on resource allocations rather than reactive decisions. Based on data from LSEG Workspace, the number of divestment transactions from the 1980s to 2025 moderated, but the total and average deal value continue to rise. In the 2026 Deloitte Global Divestiture Survey, the leading driver of divestments was the motivation to sharpen strategic focus, redeploy capital, and improve operating model efficiency, compared with the 2024 survey, where regulatory shifts and competitive pressures dominated.

We study the long-term market and accounting performance for two types of corporate divestments: spin-offs and sell-offs. The research questions are: (1) Do spin-offs and sell-offs improve long-run performance following a divestment? (2) Do market returns consistently reflect accounting performance in the long run following a divestment?

We hypothesise that sell-off companies experience greater long-term performance improvements than spin-off companies (H1). We also hypothesise that divestment provides a shock to the long-run relationship between market and accounting equity (H3) and argue that market returns and accounting performance remain consistent in the long run (H2), although investors may adjust their valuation approach to individual accounting variables following a divestment (H4).

We use a sample of 463 spin-offs and 1,345 sell-offs of US firms from 1984 to 2023. We use an event study and analyse the time series of accounting performance ratios for up to 80 quarters before and after the divestment event. To examine market perceptions of firms’ accounting performance around divestment events, we utilise Lubberink and Willett’s (2025) log-linear value-relevance model in an event study setting. The log-linear model addresses the problems of high volatility and difficult-to-interpret coefficient estimates of the traditional additive linear model. The coefficient estimates in the log-linear model are elasticities that reflect the proportionate effect of each accounting item on market value.

Consistent with H1, we show that sell-off companies experience greater long-term performance improvements than spin-offs. Abstract

For nearly a century, self-actualization has been one of psychology's most enchanting ideas, but also one of its most conceptually ambiguous and empirically elusive. Most of us can name someone we believe is fulfilling their potential, but self-actualization scholars has struggled to clearly articulate what that means, or how we would know. I argue that the field has been missing a fundamental question: actualizing toward what? Because no one can know their true potential in any inherent sense, we need a reference point to guide self-actualization in the meantime. I propose that this missing reference point is the ideal self.

This research asks three questions. How can self-actualization be defined so that it is both faithful to its theoretical lineage and tractable for empirical research? Can it be measured? And does it matter for how people's lives go?

I begin with a review of eight major perspectives on self-actualization, showing that the literature has accumulated a rich array of theoretical contentions about what self-actualization is and how it can be achieved, while leaving the field without a shared definition or an agreed reference point. In response, I introduce ideal-self-actualization (ISA) — the conscious pursuit and experience of being or becoming one's ideal self — which integrates common conceptual threads from existing perspectives while addressing critical blind spots across them. I then test this framework using a three-wave survey of US employees over six months (N = 238), developing the Ideal-Self-Actualization Scale (ISAS) and examining how ISA relates to wellbeing over time.

The findings suggest that ISA coheres as a brief and stable measure, and that it captures something existing measures of wellbeing and self-actualization do not. They also reveal a layer of self-actualization that the ISAS alone cannot reach: being one's ideal self and becoming one's ideal self turn out to be two distinct experiences, each with its own consequences for wellbeing.

This research makes three categories of contribution. Conceptually, it offers what is to my knowledge the first comparative review of eight major self-actualization perspectives, and proposes ideal-self-actualization as a pathway forward anchored to a single reference point. Methodologically, it provides a brief and psychometrically sound measure of ISA. Nomologically, it provides what is to my knowledge the first longitudinal evidence that self-actualization prospectively predicts life satisfaction. Together, these contributions open self-actualization — long an enchanting idea for psychology — to empirical inquiry.
